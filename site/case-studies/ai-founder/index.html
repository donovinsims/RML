The system: 6 steps, from research to the week after a hit

Research goes in, and the reactions tell us what to write next: a Part 2, a remix, or a tighter list.

1. The Founder Cheatsheet: one questionnaire instead of weekly interviews

A busy founder doesn't have time for weekly interviews, and most of what you need is already on the record. So send one questionnaire, then do the research yourself:

  • Podcast interviews, saved as transcripts so you can search them
  • Old blog posts, articles and their personal site
  • The posts the founder considers their best

Put the answers and the research on one page. The goals answer also tells you who to write for.

How we applied it: his goal was growing his businesses, so we wrote for 3 audiences: the ecommerce brands his software company sells to, founders raising money, and anyone building a career.

Here are the questions, so you can copy them:

  1. Goals: What does success look like in 3 months? In 12 months?
  2. Brands you respect: Which 1 to 5 personal brands would you emulate, and what do you like about each one?
  3. Topics: What 3 to 5 topics should the account cover, and which matter most?
  4. Sources: Links to the interviews, articles and old posts we can draw from.
  5. Achievements: What would make a good headline? Don't be humble, and use numbers.
  6. Life-changing events: One-liners we can turn into stories.
  7. Mistakes: "Be as vulnerable as you can here."
  8. CTA: Where should engagement go?

2. The Remix Rule: turn old posts into new ones

Old posts hold ideas already in the founder's words. Remix the short ones that didn't get their full story.

  1. Find a short post with a strong idea, like a list of one-liners.
  2. Keep the hook and the idea.
  3. Add what it was missing: the stories and the numbers.

How we applied it: his biggest post started as a list of 7 lessons he'd posted 2 months before we started, one line each. We kept his opener and gave each line a story.

3. The 3-Rank Idea Bank: know what to write next and how to write it

Keep the ideas in one database. Give each 3 ranks and a note, then write the top one first:

  • Founder rank: what the founder is excited to say, so approvals come quickly.
  • Writer rank: whether there's a hook and a proof story.
  • Audience rank: whether the topic or format has done well before.
  • The note: a few words on what to change, plus a link to the post it builds on, like "Expand on these points" or "Reframe hook."

4. Lesson, Story, Number: give each one-liner proof only the founder has

Anyone can write a bulleted lesson, so they come out generic. Give each point 3 parts:

  1. Lesson: the one-liner, in the founder's words.
  2. Story: a moment from their life when it proved true.
  3. Number: one figure that shows it happened.

How we applied it: "Never waste a good crisis" became: "In 2018, our growth stalled from 100% to 15% YoY growth... 2 years later we sold the company after our highest profit year ever." When a lesson had no story yet, the research filled it: "With $750k in funding, we scaled [his first company] to $100M+ in revenue."

5. Write 15, Post 10: cut the points anyone else could have written

A list is only as strong as its weakest point. So draft 15 points for a post of 10, then cut 5.

The test: cover the founder's name. If another expert could have written the point, cut it.

How we applied it: "Founders who don't listen" could have come from any investor, so it went. "First-time founders obsess over features. Second-time founders obsess over distribution." could only come from someone who has started 2 companies, so it stayed.

6. Use the Accordion Method: reach new people with a hit, then keep them with the founder's story

A post that hits opens the accordion wide, and a lot of new people see the founder for the first time. Then you close it in. For the next 4 days, post the founder's story so the new people stay.

How we applied it: his career advice post hit on a Monday. Here's his week:

  • Day 2, Part 2: more from the same list. Ours was 10 more pieces of career advice.
  • Day 3, the reintroduction: who the founder is, using the template below.
  • Day 4, a personal story: the person behind the advice. Ours was the decade he spent sacrificing his health and family for his first company.
  • Day 5, a remix: a post that worked before, rewritten. We kept the best lines of his Ironman post, cut the setup to 3 short lines, and turned his excuses into a list.

Here's the reintroduction template, with each part labeled:

  1. Open on the hit in plain words.
  2. Welcome the new people.
  3. Say who you are in one line.
  4. Tell the origin story with 1 or 2 numbers.
  5. List what you're working on now.
  6. Add one personal detail.
  7. Close with an invite.
