How we wrote tweets for a busy CEO without ever interviewing them

For about a year, we were the copywriter for a busy CEO's X account. They approved 469 of our tweets, and 446 went live on their account. Those posts did nearly 26 million impressions and 953,701 likes without us interviewing them once.

Where we started

They were the CEO of a big company. Which means they were busy, and there was no time for weekly interviews.

So we never talked to them about their tweets. Our job was to learn their voice well enough that they'd read a tweet and think, "yep, I'd say that."

How we thought through the solution

Tweets are the most concise way to express an idea. You're compressing an idea into 280 characters, and you can't compress an idea you don't have.

So with a busy founder, you find what they've already said and compress it.

Their ideas were everywhere: podcast episodes, YouTube videos, internal meetings, workshops, and messages to their team. So I wouldn't say we wrote their tweets completely on their behalf. It was more like remixing things we knew they'd said and valued before.

The best way to write high-performing new tweets is to look at high-performing old ones. Their old tweets showed me what their audience already liked. Their values told me what they'd say again and again.

Then there's trust. They needed to read a tweet and know it was theirs, in a few seconds, without asking me where it came from.

So we put every tweet next to the words it came from.

Our process

1. Listed every place they already talk

We pulled from podcast episodes, a YouTube video, a media team feedback meeting, an internal workshop, and a message they'd sent their team.

We labeled each source, like "old proven tweets," "affiliate workshop quote," "idea from sales handbook" or "from livestream."

2. Studied their top tweets

We made a big list of their top 20 tweets of all time. Then we built a sheet of their top 499, sorted by likes and tagged by theme.

Why both? Sorting by likes shows you what worked. Tagging by theme shows you why.

They had already posted "The price of success is hate." and "The price of authenticity is being disliked." as one-liners, and both had done well. In our first batch, we stacked them and added a third line:

Our breakdown of 2,116 posts found short text (under 280 characters) had the highest average likes and reposts of any format, and long single posts beat threads. Their 10 most-viewed approved tweets were all under 280 characters, with no link.

We put the phrases they repeated into a words bank, so the next draft used their words instead of mine.

3. Figured out their core values

Values are magnets. Your values attract people who believe in the same things.

On top of that, they're repeatable. They make it easy to say the same idea in 1,000 different ways. If you look at an account's best posts, they're usually 5 or 6 values said over and over again.

To find them, we answered 4 questions from what they'd said on their podcast, in their livestreams, and in their old tweets:

  • What's their personal quest?
  • What do they believe strongly that most people don't?
  • What pisses them off?
  • What hill are they willing to (metaphorically) die on?

Some of the values we identified:

  • F*ck your mood, follow the plan
  • Do hard things
  • Fall in love with boring
  • Choose your friends and partner wisely
  • Lead yourself first

They'd been posting "F*ck your mood, follow the plan" for years, so we kept finding new ways to say it. By my count, at least 17 of their tweets that went live were some version of it, and together they did about 3.7 million impressions.

One of them, "One of the most underrated skills you can learn is the ability to ignore your mood and stick with the plan," did 1.2 million impressions on its own.

4. Used a remixing checklist

The most quotable people don't say new things. They say obvious things in non-obvious ways.

There are 2 main ways to remix:

  • Same structure, different topic
  • Same topic, different structure

For example, if we said, "Rich people buy time. Poor people buy stuff."

Same structure, different topic: "Winners get better. Losers get bitter."

Same topic, different structure: "If you want to build wealth, buy time. If you want to stay poor, buy stuff."

Batch 1 had "The quickest way to become more confident is to become more competent." In batch 3, we kept the structure and changed the words:

Other high-performing templates we used:

  • If you want X, do Y
  • You don't need X, you need Y
  • X is overrated. Y is underrated
  • The older I get, the more I...
  • The fastest way to __ is to ___
  • Lack of X has killed more dreams than Y
  • Life improves dramatically when you ___

Pro tip: if you want to write mega bangers, take 2 bangers and combine them.

5. Put the source next to every tweet

Starting with batch 5, we put every one of their tweets right next to its source, "so you know it is your words/voice," as the doc put it.

Why does this matter? A tweet with no source makes them ask, "Did I say this?" A tweet next to their own words answers that before they ask. So their few minutes go to approving.

6. Added specific details to generic tweets

When a tweet is generic, you have 2 options:

  • Chunk up: go broader, to the bigger principle.
  • Chunk down: add details the reader can picture.

We chunked down more, because founders approve specific tweets.

"Move fast" is vague. "The fastest way to spot a successful person: watch how quickly they make decisions, respond to messages, and turn ideas into action" gives the reader 3 actions they can picture someone doing.

For every vague word, we ask ourselves, "what the hell do we mean?"

FixBeforeAfter

Before anything went in a batch, we ran it through a short checklist:

  • What's the one idea?
  • Who is it for?
  • Could we say it in fewer words? Simpler words?
  • Can we use a more specific number or timeframe?
  • Does line 1 make them read line 2?
  • Could any guru have written it? If so, tie it to the founder's method or mistake.

7. Used psychology and rhetorical devices

One of my favorite ways to make a tweet perform better is "Aspirational Identity Framing." This is when you describe a behavior through the lens of a group people want to belong to:

  • Describe a desirable identity (successful, high performer, disciplined, rich).
  • List behaviors that signal that identity.
  • Readers compare themselves to that standard.
  • If they aspire to that identity, they lean in and engage.

So instead of saying "Move fast and get things done quickly," we wrote this:

Rhetorical devices are techniques writers use to persuade, get an emotional reaction, or make a message more memorable.

One example is antithetical parallelism. You repeat a sentence structure ("Lack of X has killed more dreams than lack of Y"), which creates rhythm. Then you contrast 2 opposing ideas inside it ("focus" vs. "talent") to show which one matters.

So instead of "Focus is more important than talent," you write "Lack of focus has killed more dreams than lack of talent." That one went live on their account:

8. Used proven hooks and wrote 3 first lines for every tweet

On X, the first line's only job is to get the second line read.

Short text posts work best: 1 to 4 lines, one idea, no link. Put 2 ideas in one tweet and the reader gets confused (and you can't tell which one landed).

For every tweet, we wrote 3 options and checked each one 4 ways:

  • Benefit: why should they care?
  • Relevance: who is it for?
  • Credibility: why listen to this founder?
  • Perceived effort: how fast and easy does it feel to read?

Then I ran the Name Test: cover the founder's name and photo. Could someone tell it was them? If not, it's too generic.

The hook formulas are great to start with. The goal is a hook that passes the Name Test without one.

Formatting rules we held every tweet to:

  • Use single-line paragraphs. Start short, then go longer once you have their attention.
  • End on the strongest line, and don't explain the punchline.
  • Use sentence case or all lowercase (if that's the founder's style). Don't Capitalize Every Word.
  • Don't use em dashes. Readers now see them as a sign of AI.
  • Put links in the reply. In our breakdown, a link in the main post cut likes by about two-thirds.

10. Said the same idea in different ways

Once you know an angle works, the question becomes: how much can you double down without the audience getting bored?

"Sooner or later you realize everything comes down to your ability to stick with the plan despite whatever emotions you may feel." did 361K impressions.

A later batch said it again: "You become an adult when you realize everything eventually comes down to your ability to stick with what you said you'd do despite whatever emotions you may feel." That one did 288K.

We also stacked old winners into one post. This list reused at least 3 lines they'd already posted and done well with: "Fuck your mood. Follow the plan.", "The price of authenticity is being disliked." and "Your new life will cost you your old one."

Batch 4 repeated their past winners and added relationship tweets, because relationship content was working for them on Instagram. I also left myself a note in the doc: "Pull more from lives than old tweets."

Each post does one of 3 jobs:

  • Reach posts pull in new people with broad advice.
  • Authority posts are tactical breakdowns.
  • Resonance posts are the founder's story, mistakes, and life outside work, like the relationship tweets.

Founders tend to skip the personal posts, so we kept them in the plan on purpose.

We also used what we call the 3Rs:

  • Repost a winner about 90 days later. Most of the audience didn't see it the first time.
  • Repurpose it onto other platforms: a LinkedIn post, a newsletter section, a short video script.
  • Remix it with a new template, or combine 2 winners into one.

The speed tweet I wrote did 326K views the first time. When we posted it again later, it did another 180K, with 10K likes against 11K the first time:

That lines up with our X training guide: reposted winners regularly land at 60% to 100% of the original's likes. We call this "test, then invest."

To know which tweets to repost, we kept a Hall of Fame: each post that beat the account's average likes or impressions, with the date, topic, template and why it won.

End state

446 of the 469 tweets they approved went live, with nearly 26 million impressions and 953,701 likes. And we never talked to them about any of them.

By the later batches, we put every tweet next to its source, they approved everything in one doc, and their bolded tweets told me what to write next.

At RML, these are now standing rules:

  • The founder approves in batch once a week.
  • Their edits come first on 2 questions: is it true, and does it sound like them?
  • We log every cut in a "why it got cut" doc.
  • We never publish a number, name, or claim the founder hasn't confirmed.
